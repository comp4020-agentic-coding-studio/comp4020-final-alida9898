# What multi-agent coding is for

Whether coding work should be split across several agents is one of the open arguments in agentic development. I take the position that the argument is usually framed around the wrong question. Both sides treat multi-agent systems as a way to get more work done at once, and then disagree about whether that pays. The better reason to split work between agents is to divide responsibility: each agent owns one part, has a written boundary it must not cross, and has its work checked by an agent that did not build it. Judged that way, a multi-agent setup is worth it when its parts are genuinely independent and its checks are honest about what they cannot see, and not otherwise.

The case against is put most sharply by Walden Yan at Cognition (2025). When a Flappy Bird clone is split between one subagent building the pipes and another building the bird, each fills the gaps in its brief with its own guesses and the halves do not fit, because "Actions carry implicit decisions, and conflicting decisions carry bad results." Yan's answer is a single agent that keeps the whole context. Anthropic, describing its own multi-agent research system, comes close to agreeing for code: such systems use "about 15× more tokens than chats", and "most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research" (Hadfield et al., 2025). The academic record is no kinder. Cemri et al. (2025) annotated over 1,600 traces from seven multi-agent frameworks and found that gains on benchmarks are "often minimal", with failures clustering in system design, misalignment between agents, and task verification.

These sources are not neutral. Cognition sells a coding agent and Anthropic is paid by the token, and both measure multi-agent systems by output and speed. Read that way, Yan's example shows something narrower than his title. The bird and the pipes share one screen and one art style; they were one task cut in half, so the implicit decisions he worries about were bound to collide. Anthropic's own account points to the alternative. Its early orchestrator gave short, vague instructions, and subagents duplicated work and misread their tasks, so each now receives "an objective, an output format, guidance on the tools and sources to use, and clear task boundaries" (Hadfield et al., 2025). Schluntz and Zhang (2024) separate "sectioning", "breaking a task into independent subtasks", from an orchestrator that decides at run time what to hand out. Their distinction, together with the boundaries Anthropic added after its own failures, is about who owns what, not about how many hands are working.

My own project gives a small test. I built a multi-user 3D town with fixed agents for cameras, 3D models, interface text, phones, the server and checking, each listed in CLAUDE.md with what it owns and what it must not touch. Their jobs rarely overlap, because the shape of a model and the camera that frames it really are separate decisions. Where they did overlap, the cause was a boundary drawn too late: seasonal weather was built before any agent owned it, and the line had to be redrawn afterwards.

The weaker half of my position is checking. Cemri et al. list verification as one of the three main ways multi-agent systems fail, and checking by another model has known limits. Zheng et al. (2023) found that LLM judges agree with human preferences over 80% of the time but show position, verbosity and self-enhancement biases. Speed is not settled either: in a randomised trial, experienced developers using AI tools were 19% slower while believing they had been 20% faster (Becker et al., 2025). Anthropic's own conclusion is that "even in a world of automated evaluations, manual testing remains essential" (Hadfield et al., 2025). In my project a checker once reported every test passing when one failed on a second run, and no agent could judge whether the town's cat looked right; it was fixed only when I tuned it myself with sliders. A checker earns its place only if it is small enough to finish, is itself checked, and says plainly what it cannot judge.

Multi-agent coding is not justified by speed, which is expensive and poorly evidenced. It is justified by ownership: independent parts, written boundaries and a checker that knows its limits. Where a task cannot be divided along those lines, Yan is right, and a single agent with the whole context is the better choice.
